Your options

Types of IRS payment plans

The right plan depends on how much you owe and what you can afford each month.

Short-term payment plan

Extra time, up to 180 days, to pay your balance in full. There is no setup fee, though interest and penalties continue until it's paid.

Long-term installment agreement

Monthly payments over a longer period. Smaller balances can often be set up with less paperwork, and paying by direct debit lowers the setup fee.

Non-streamlined agreement

For larger balances, the IRS asks for a detailed financial statement to decide what monthly amount you can afford.

Partial payment installment agreement

If you can't pay the full balance before the IRS collection period ends, the IRS may accept smaller payments that won't cover everything you owe.

What a payment plan costs

Interest keeps accruing on the unpaid balance, and the failure-to-pay penalty continues at a reduced rate while the agreement is in effect. Setup fees vary by plan type and payment method, and may be reduced or waived for low-income taxpayers.

What can cause a default

Missing payments, owing new taxes you can't pay, or not filing future returns can put your agreement in default. If that happens, the IRS may resume collection, so it's important to act quickly if your finances change.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions

How much do I need to owe to get an IRS payment plan?+

There is no minimum. Short-term plans and streamlined long-term plans are available for many balances, and larger balances can still qualify with a financial review.

Will a payment plan stop a wage garnishment or bank levy?+

The IRS generally won't start a new levy while an installment agreement request is pending or approved, and an approved agreement is often grounds to release an existing wage levy.

Can I change my payment amount later?+

Yes. If your finances change, you can ask the IRS to revise your installment agreement. It's better to do this before you miss a payment.
